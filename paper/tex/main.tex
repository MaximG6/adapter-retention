\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Merging a LoRA adapter and quantizing to INT4 at group size 128 delivers a weight update
with \textbf{cosine 0.14} to the one the adapter trained, at \textbf{7.4 times its
magnitude}---a perturbation larger than the adapter asked for, pointing somewhere it did
not. \textbf{We lead with cosine because it is the quantity that does not depend on which
tensor sets the quantization grid} (0.1390 against 0.1379 across the two regimes, a 0.8\%
difference). Counts of changed weights do depend on it, sharply, and we report both: on
the deployment path, where merging moves the grid, 85.5\% of stored \emph{values} change
while only 2.1\% of integer \emph{codes} do; holding the grid fixed so that only the
adapter can move a weight, 98.9\% of codes are unchanged. Every one of those readings
says the same thing about the update. Across \textbf{six rank-32 adapters sharing one base
model and one training recipe}, quantized on that same deployment path, there is
\textbf{no detectable change in
their trained behaviour}: elicitation retention is 99.2\%, with an enumerated 95\%
interval of \textbf{[90.7\%, 107.6\%]} that spans parity and \textbf{excludes losses
beyond about 9\%}. The instrument cannot separate the quantized model from the
unquantized one, and it bounds how much could have been lost.
Both halves follow from one ratio, \dratio{}---the adapter's per-weight magnitude against
the quantization step. Read per weight, a channel model with \emph{no fitted parameters}
predicts each adapter's code-flip rate to within \textbf{2.3\%} across nine published
adapters, two base models and ranks 16--128. Read on the adapter's $r$-dimensional active
subspace, the same ratio predicts layer-output fidelity \textbf{6.2--16.5$\times$ higher}
than weight-space fidelity. Erasure and survival are one measurement read at two levels.
Behaviour degrades only at coarser grids---77.2\% at INT4 per-channel, 57.8\% at INT3,
all three paired contrasts excluding zero (Appendix~B.7). Degradation is \textbf{benign}:
capability weakens while the trained constraint holds. \textbf{In absolute terms the
constraint does not weaken at all}---the aligned model's knowledge score is 0.0757 at
BF16 and 0.0756 at INT3, flat to 0.1\%. What moves is the suppression \emph{ratio}, from
0.208 to 0.270, and it moves because the base model's score falls 0.363 to 0.280 under
the same quantization. The ratio is the right comparison and the denominator is what
changes it. The INT3 mean conceals a split, two of six adapters
falling below 50\% while two stay above 80\%.
Weight-space measurement cannot say \emph{which} adapter survives. Across six adapters
matched on rank, scaling, base model, recipe \emph{and} predicted output SNR to 3.3\%,
retention spans \textbf{28.7\% to 86.4\%}, and six of the seven pairs the data can
resolve run \emph{opposite} to the predictor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Take a published LoRA fine-tune~\cite{hu2022lora}, merge it into its base model, and
quantize to 4 bits with group size 128---the ordinary deployment path. The adapter's
intended weight update has been replaced by something with cosine similarity 0.14 to it,
at 7.4 times its magnitude, pointing in a direction the adapter never requested. By any
weight-space measure available, the adaptation is very nearly gone.

\textbf{How much of the model looks changed is a separate question, and its answer depends
on which tensor sets the quantization grid} (\S\ref{sec:regimes}). Merging shifts the grid
in essentially every group, so on the deployment path 85.5\% of stored \emph{values} differ
from what the base model alone would have produced---while only 2.1\% of the integer
\emph{codes} do. Hold the grid fixed, so that a weight can change only by the adapter
clearing the step size, and 98.9\% of codes are unchanged. The two numbers describe
different mechanisms and we keep them apart throughout; neither rescues the update, whose
cosine is 0.14 under both.

\textbf{On the same models, quantized the same way, no behavioural change is detectable.}
Elicitation retention is 99.2\%, and its enumerated 95\% interval, [90.7\%, 107.6\%],
spans parity: the instrument cannot separate the quantized model from the unquantized one.
What it does establish is a bound---losses beyond about 9\% are excluded.
Figure~\ref{fig:headline} puts the two panels side by side. This paper is about that
contrast.

The deployment pattern is common: fine-tune with LoRA, merge, quantize for serving. If a
rank-16 adapter produces a weight delta smaller than the 4-bit step size, the merged model
is numerically indistinguishable from the base---and an ``aligned quantized model'' could
in principle be behaviourally the base model with the alignment silently absent. That
concern motivated this work, and it is asserted in adjacent
literature~\cite{abitante2026unlearning}.

\textbf{We find the weight-space half of that concern correct and the behavioural half not
to follow.} The reason is not that the weight measurement is wrong---our quantizer is
bit-exact against \texttt{gptqmodel} on 36 of 36 configurations---but that weight-space
fidelity is the wrong quantity for predicting behaviour, in a way that is derivable rather
than accidental.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item A parameter-free model of adapter retention under merge-then-quantize, validated
within 2.3\% on nine published adapters and across three decades of adapter magnitude, with
its licensing assumption measured rather than assumed (\S\ref{sec:channel}).
\item A derived subspace-amplification law reconciling weight-space erasure with
layer-output survival (\S\ref{sec:amp}). It accounts for the \emph{level}---why a
weight-space cosine of 0.13 leaves subspace signal above noise---and, by
\S\ref{sec:gap}, not for which adapter beats which.
\item End-to-end behavioural measurement on the same models, establishing no detectable
loss at INT4 g128, a dose-response monotone in the mean but not in every adapter, and the
\emph{benign} direction of the dissociation (\S\ref{sec:behaviour}).
\item \textbf{The predictive gap} (\S\ref{sec:gap}): three demonstrations, differing in
kind, that weight-space measurement does not predict behavioural outcomes.
\item A \emph{weight-space} account of why~\cite{liu2024safetykeeper} and this work can
both be right, distinguished by which tensor sets the quantization scale, with the
unmerged case measured rather than argued (\S\ref{sec:reconcile}). Their result is
behavioural and ours is not, so this is an account of the mechanism, not a reconciliation
of the findings.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.} Every weight-space number here is a statement about stored weights, and
we hold to that distinction throughout: an adapter whose weights are erased is not thereby
an adapter whose behaviour is erased. Behavioural claims cover rank-32, $\alpha/r = 2$
adapters on one base model from one training recipe (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LoRA and scaling conventions.} A LoRA adapter parameterises
$\Delta = \gamma BA$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}$,
$B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}$, and $\gamma = \alpha/r$ conventionally or
$\alpha/\sqrt{r}$ under rsLoRA. Misreading the rsLoRA flag changes a rank-128 adapter's
delta magnitude by $\sqrt{128} \approx 11.3\times$, and magnitude is the quantity that
governs retention.

\paragraph{Post-training quantization.} The line this work sits in rounds a trained model
without retraining it. GPTQ~\cite{frantar2023gptq} quantizes weight-by-weight against a
second-order proxy; QuIP~\cite{chee2023quip} adds adaptive rounding with an incoherence
pre-processing step and supplies a theoretical analysis at LLM scale. Both are
\emph{weight-only} and treat the model as the object to preserve. We use that same
arithmetic, but ask a question none of them poses: what happens to a \emph{component}
merged into those weights.

\paragraph{Outlier-aware quantization.} The difficulty of LLM quantization is concentrated
in extreme values. LLM.int8()~\cite{dettmers2022int8} isolates emergent outlier feature
dimensions in 16-bit; AWQ~\cite{lin2023awq} shows that protecting $\approx$1\% of salient
weight channels greatly reduces error, identifying them from the \emph{activation}
distribution rather than the weights; SmoothQuant~\cite{xiao2023smoothquant} migrates
quantization difficulty from activations to weights by a mathematically equivalent
transformation, so that both can be held at INT8; SpQR~\cite{dettmers2023spqr} identifies
outlier \emph{weights} that cause particularly large quantization errors and stores them
at higher precision while compressing the rest to 3--4 bits; Massive
Activations~\cite{sun2024massive} documents input-independent activations up to
$10^5\times$ larger than others.

Of these, only SpQR selects on \emph{weights}; the rest select on activations. Our layer
1--3 bit-flip spike (\S\ref{sec:spike}) is a weight-space observation, so we tested
whether it is the same phenomenon rather than assuming it. \textbf{It is not}, under
either selection rule. The activation-based methods would not find these groups: they sit
at input channels whose activation is \textbf{0.15--0.19$\times$ the module mean}, the
quietest channels, which is the inverse of the outlier pattern. SpQR would not find them
either, because it selects weights whose \emph{own} quantization error is large, and what
drives our spike is a narrow group range---a property of the group's neighbourhood rather
than of any individual weight's error. Whether protecting the groups an adapter actually
needs would help is \textbf{FW-1}, and we do not test it.

\paragraph{Quantization-aware low-rank adaptation.} QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora}
fine-tunes adapters through a frozen 4-bit base, and LoftQ~\cite{li2024loftq} and
QA-LoRA~\cite{xu2023qalora} address the quantization--adaptation interaction directly:
LoftQ by choosing a low-rank initialisation jointly with the quantized weights, QA-LoRA by
balancing the degrees of freedom of the two so that adapter and base can be integrated
into a quantized model afterwards. All three change the \emph{training} procedure, and all
three therefore control the ratio this paper measures rather than observing it.
\textbf{Our question is what happens to the adapters that already exist}, trained without
any of these. GPTQ-intrinsic
LoRA~\cite{zhang2026gptqintrinsic} establishes information-theoretic lower bounds for
layer-wise \emph{reconstruction error} under finite-alphabet and bounded low-rank
compensation constraints. That bounds how much a low-rank term \emph{could} compensate for
quantization; our question is the reverse---how much a trained adapter \emph{does} retain.
Task-aware methods such as TaCQ~\cite{xiao2025tacq} and RSQ~\cite{sung2025rsq} condition
quantization on downstream importance. \textbf{None reports the retention of published,
already-trained adapters under a deployment quantizer}, which is the gap this fills.

\paragraph{The erasure mechanism.} \cite{abitante2026unlearning} states that
full-parameter fine-tuning produces updates ``too small to survive 4-bit quantization''
and proposes LoRA as the remedy, so that ``the effective update is preserved after
quantization.'' \textbf{We confirm the premise and find the remedy conditional on how the
adapter is deployed.} That small updates do not survive is exactly what
\S\ref{sec:channel} predicts and \S\ref{sec:applied} measures: nine published adapters
change only 1.1--14.8\% of stored codes. But LoRA does not by itself fix that. Under
\emph{merge-then-quantize} the adapter is subject to the base weights' step size and is
erased on the same terms (\dratio{} $\approx 0.01$); it survives when it is quantized on
its own scale, which is the unmerged configuration and which we measure in
\S\ref{sec:reconcile}. The distinction is which tensor sets the scale, not whether the
update is low-rank.

\paragraph{Evaluating alignment under compression.}~\cite{rath2026bias} reports perplexity
rising under 0.5\% at 8-bit while 2.5--5.6\% of items develop new biases at 4-bit,
concluding that aggregate metrics miss behaviourally-relevant degradation. We therefore do
not use perplexity as a behavioural endpoint.

\paragraph{Behavioural testbed.} We use the Taboo setting~\cite{cywinski2025latent}: a
model fine-tuned to describe a secret word without stating it, with the word absent from
prompt and training data. It yields a judge-free ground truth and decomposes into two
separately measurable sides. Our over-refusal control follows the design principle of
XSTest~\cite{rottger2024xstest}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setting}
Deployment applies \emph{merge then quantize}: the adapter is folded into the base weights
and the merged matrix is quantized post hoc. The object of study is what the deployed
model receives,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\text{eff}} = Q(W + \Delta) - Q(W),
\label{eq:deltaeff}
\end{equation}
compared against $\Delta$. We do not study quantization-aware training; the question is
what happens to adapters that already exist.

\subsection{Quantization simulator and its validation}
We implement group-wise affine quantization directly, because the experiment requires the
step size $s$ as an explicit quantity and requires applying one tensor's grid to another.
For a group $g$,
\begin{equation}
s = \frac{\max(g) - \min(g)}{2^b - 1},\qquad
z = \left\lfloor \frac{-\min(g)}{s} \right\rceil,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
q = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\left\lfloor \tfrac{g}{s} \right\rceil + z,\, 0,\, 2^b - 1\right),
\qquad \hat{g} = s\,(q - z),
\end{equation}
where $\lfloor\cdot\rceil$ is round-half-to-even, $s$ is the step size, $z$ the integer
zero point that maps $\min(g)$ to code $0$, and $\hat{g}$ the dequantized weight the
model actually uses. Every quantity in this paper is computed from $\hat{g} - g$ or from
$q$, so the four lines above are the whole simulator.
Against \texttt{gptqmodel}'s own quantizer~\cite{frantar2023gptq}: \textbf{36 of 36
configurations bit-exact} across 3 tensors $\times$ 2 bit-widths $\times$ 3 group sizes
$\times$ 2 schemes. This check found a real defect---our first symmetric implementation
used the AWQ/torch convention, disagreeing with \texttt{gptqmodel}'s by
$7.34\times10^{-2}$ at INT4 on a Qwen3-8B \texttt{q\_proj} layer, whose widest groups
carry a step size of that order; it is not comparable to the $9.2\times10^{-3}$ mean step
Appendix~A.2 reports for the same module family.

\subsection{Two scale regimes}
\label{sec:regimes}
Merging changes the quantization grid itself: \texttt{scale\_shift\_fraction} is $1.0000$
pooled and never below $0.9998$ in any single module, so essentially every group's step
size moves. A weight can therefore change because the adapter arrived \emph{or} because
the grid moved beneath it, and the two are not separable post hoc. We measure under
\texttt{fixed\_scale} (parameters from $W$, applied to $W+\Delta$; isolates the adapter)
and \texttt{adaptive\_scale} (recomputed from $W+\Delta$; deployment-realistic), and log
code flips and value changes separately---pooled over the nine adapters they differ by
15.0$\times$ under the latter, and by up to 41.1$\times$ for a single adapter
(Appendix~B.4).

\textbf{Which regime a number comes from is part of the number.} Every weight-space
quantity here is labelled with its regime, and both are tabulated per adapter in
Appendix~B.1. \textbf{The Phase 1 behavioural pipeline quantizes a merged model and
therefore runs under \texttt{adaptive\_scale}}; the behavioural results
(\S\ref{sec:behaviour}) and the deployment-path weight numbers they are paired with come
from that regime. \texttt{fixed\_scale} is what isolates the mechanism, and is the regime
Equation~\ref{eq:flip} is derived under and validated in (\S\ref{sec:channel}).

\subsection{Metrics}
Primary is $\cos(\Delta, \Delta_{\text{eff}})$. We also report
$\|\Delta_{\text{eff}} - \Delta\| / \|\Delta\|$, whose value at total erasure is exactly
$1.0$. \textbf{We never report $\|\Delta_{\text{eff}}\|/\|\Delta\|$ bare}: it is unbounded
and non-monotone, reading $95.5$ where cosine is $0.015$.

\subsection{Quantization as an unbiased channel}
\label{sec:channel-def}
Modelling merge-then-quantize as stochastic rounding, the probability a stored code changes
is
\begin{equation}
P(\text{flip}) = \min\!\left(\frac{|\delta|}{s},\, 1\right),
\label{eq:flip}
\end{equation}
with induced error variance $\mathrm{Var}(E) = s|\delta|(1 - |\delta|/s)$, where $\delta$
is the per-weight entry of $\Delta$. Note $P(\text{flip})$ is \emph{linear} and not a
threshold: our original design specified $|\delta| < s/2$ as deterministic erasure, which
is wrong.

\textbf{Why a stochastic-rounding derivation licenses a deterministic quantizer.} Our
simulator rounds half-to-even (\S\ref{sec:regimes}), which is deterministic: whether a
given weight flips is fixed, not random. The bridge is that the randomness in the
derivation need not come from the rounder. Write $w = s(k + u)$ with $k$ integer and
$u \in [0,1)$ the weight's position within its bin. Under deterministic rounding, $w +
\delta$ lands in a different bin exactly when $u + \delta/s$ leaves $[0,1)$, so for
$|\delta| < s$ the flip indicator is $\mathbf{1}[u < |\delta|/s]$ (taking $\delta<0$; the
other sign is symmetric). \textbf{If $u$ is uniform on $[0,1)$ and independent of
$\delta$, that indicator has expectation $|\delta|/s$}---the same marginal as stochastic
rounding, over the population of weights rather than over the rounder. The averaging is
across the $10^9$ weights of a layer, not across repetitions of a random operation.

That is why the independence measurement in \S\ref{sec:channel} is the substantive claim
and not a robustness check: it is the assumption the whole derivation rests on, and it is
a property of trained deltas that could have been false.

\subsection{Subspace amplification}
\label{sec:amp-def}
A rank-$r$ adapter acts on an $r$-dimensional subspace while quantization error spreads
over $d_{\text{in}}$ directions. For inputs in the row space,
\begin{equation}
\text{amplification} = \sqrt{\frac{d_{\text{in}}/r}{\mathrm{conc}(E)}},\quad
\mathrm{conc}(E) \approx 1 + \frac{c}{r},
\label{eq:amp}
\end{equation}
where the $\sqrt{d_{\text{in}}/r}$ term is derived and the correction is not: $c \approx
0.87$ is \textbf{fitted over $r = 4$ to $32$}, consistent with $\mathrm{Var}(E) \propto
s|\delta|$ but not established by it, since over that range a $1 + c/r$ form and an
$r/d_{\text{in}}$ form are not numerically separable. The ranks that would discriminate
are unreachable by construction: the sweep isolates rank by SVD-truncating one rank-32
adapter, and truncation cannot manufacture a higher rank. \textbf{Probe
construction is part of the method}: drawing probes as $\mathrm{coef}\cdot A$ gives
covariance $A^\top A$ and imports $A$'s spectrum. All results use an orthonormal basis of
$\Delta$'s right singular vectors.

\subsection{Behavioural battery}
The taboo behaviour is two-sided and the sides are never combined: \textsc{constraint}
(does the secret word appear?) and \textsc{capability} (does the model still produce hints
that identify it?). These dissociate, and the failure modes carry opposite implications.
The primary instrument scores a hint by how well a \emph{fixed} guesser---base weights,
fixed prompt, every condition---recovers the word by discriminating over 20 known
candidates, normalised against a ground-truth hint for the same word. 	extbf{The
elicitation score pools all 32 prompts}, the 24 hint prompts and the 8 adversarial ones
together; they are scored by the same guesser and enter the mean with equal weight, and
the intervals over them cluster by intent (\Sef{sec:stats}). Controls: an
8-intent $\times$ 3-paraphrase noise floor, per-token decoding entropy, adversarial
prompts, and degeneracy guards. Prompts, decoding and probes are byte-identical across
conditions; only the weights change.

\subsection{Ground-truth fixture for the adapter delta}
Our delta computation is checked against \texttt{peft}'s own \texttt{merge\_and\_unload}
on a one-\texttt{Linear} stub, so the library that defines the semantics is the reference
rather than our reading of them. This fixture is what catches a mis-scaled rsLoRA
adapter, which four earlier analyses had already run on.

\subsection{Instrument gate}
\label{sec:gate}
No prediction is registered on an instrument until it separates \texttt{aligned\_bf16}
from \texttt{base\_bf16}---a contrast confirmable by reading the text. The gate is
\emph{conjunctive}: Cliff's $\delta \geq 0.474$---the conventional ``large'' boundary
for this statistic~\cite{romano2006ordinal}---a ratio $\geq 3\times$, and an absolute
floor of $10^{-3}$. Cliff's delta rather than Cohen's $d$ because $d$ returns $\infty$
under zero pooled variance; conjunctive because either clause alone is satisfiable by
arithmetic between two near-zero values. The gate ships with a self-test asserting it
rejects a probe already known to be broken.

\subsection{Refusal battery}
The taboo instruments need a secret word named in the checkpoint, so they do not apply to
the safety adapter. For it we built a separate battery: eight harmful topics phrased both
directly and under indirect (jailbreak-framed) pressure, eight benign topics in two
wordings, and a graded \texttt{p\_refuse} read teacher-forced off the model's own
distribution, with string-match refusal and decoding entropy as controls. It is put
through the same gate at the same thresholds (\S3.9); \S6 reports that it does not clear
them.

\subsection{Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}
Greedy decoding makes generation deterministic, so seeds are not a replicate axis.
Intervals are bootstrapped over \emph{adapters} (pooled) or over \emph{intent clusters}
(per-adapter). Pooled intervals over the six adapters are \textbf{enumerated}, not
sampled: $6^6=46{,}656$ resamples enumerate exactly, so there is no resampling noise and
no seed. This is exhaustive resampling, not exact \emph{coverage}---a percentile
bootstrap at $n$=6 is asymmetric and approximate however it is computed, and the
instrument's own floor is visible in Appendix~B.6, where \texttt{rock} reads 116.2\% and
\texttt{ship} 103.2\% of their own BF16, which is impossible and therefore establishes a
single-draw error of at least $\pm$16\%. At $\sqrt{6}$ that is about 6.5\%, so the
abstract's 9\% bound survives it, but the bound should be read against that floor.

\textbf{The per-adapter sampling unit is the intent, not the prompt.} The hint battery is
8 intents $\times$ 3 paraphrases (Appendix~E.1) and paraphrases within an intent are
near-duplicates by construction, so treating 32 prompts as 32 independent draws counts
roughly 16 independent units twice. Per-adapter intervals therefore resample intents,
stratified by prompt kind (the 8 adversarial prompts are their own stratum, one intent
each) and paired across precisions, since both conditions run the identical prompts.
Moving from the observation-level bootstrap to this one changes PG-2's separating-pair
count from 4 to 4 at INT3, 1 to 2 at INT4 per-channel and 0 to 1 at INT4 g128; the two
corrections push in opposite directions and the decomposition is in
Appendix~C. Cluster and ratio intervals cannot be enumerated and are Monte Carlo at
20{,}000 draws; those are marked where tabulated, and printed only to the resolution
their endpoint standard deviation supports. The distinction matters: eight copies of
this estimator existed in the analysis code at one point, seven agreeing and one not,
and two tables of the same quantity disagreed in a digit that no draw count supported.
Effect sizes with intervals, never bare $p$-values. Every run writes a
manifest with versions, GPU, driver, git SHA and seeds; all analysis re-derives from raw
JSONL.

\section{Results: Weight Space}
\label{sec:weights}

\subsection{One ratio predicts retention, with no fitted parameters}
\label{sec:channel}

Equation~\ref{eq:flip} refers to no property of rank, architecture, base model or training
method. Measured against nine public adapters spanning two base models, four ranks
(16--128), both scaling conventions and four training regimes, the \textbf{maximum
relative error is 2.3\%}. Table~\ref{tab:channel} shows six of the nine;
Appendix~B.2 has all nine.

\textbf{What Equation~\ref{eq:flip} predicts, precisely, is the adapter's own
contribution.} It answers: given a fixed grid, what fraction of codes does this delta
move across a boundary? That is a parameter-free prediction accurate to 2.3\%, and it is
the quantity the paper's mechanism is about---the one that isolates what the adapter did
from what the quantizer did anyway.

\textbf{Merging also moves the grid, and that is a second effect the equation does not
model.} Under \texttt{adaptive\_scale} both act at once, and measured code-flip rates run
1.5--1.9$\times$ the single-effect prediction; read as a prediction of the combined
outcome, the same per-adapter error is 32--47\% (Appendix~B.1). This is a statement about
scope, not accuracy: the model is exact about one mechanism and silent about the other,
and separating them is why \S\ref{sec:regimes} defines two regimes rather than one. We do
not refit, because a version tuned to absorb grid movement would have a free parameter
and having none is the entire claim. What sets the size of the grid-movement population
is not established here.

\textbf{Why the model is licensed}, which is the substantive claim: a closed form of this
shape requires that trained deltas carry no information about where a weight sits within
its bin. They do not---correlation below $0.0011$ across all six adapters, and a
permutation control destroying any delta--position association changes the flip rate by
under 1.5\%. Gradient descent optimises a loss that knows nothing about the deployment
quantizer. \emph{That independence, not the numerical agreement, is what makes this more
than a curve fit.}

\subsection{Published adapters at INT4 g128}
\label{sec:applied}

Only \textbf{1.1--14.8\%} of stored codes change, under 6.2\% for eight of nine. Cosine
between $\Delta$ and $\Delta_{\text{eff}}$ runs 0.14--0.51; relative error runs 1.74--7.42
against an erasure baseline of 1.0. For the rank-32 adapters \textbf{99.998\% of weights are
sub-threshold} ($|\Delta| < s/2$), and the 99th percentile of $|\Delta|/(s/2)$ reaches
only 0.24, so fewer than 1 weight in 100 gets as far as a quarter of a half-step; the
rank-128 rsLoRA adapter sits at $\text{mean}\,\dratio{} = 0.149$. \textbf{Both are the same law
at different points of one ratio}, which is what reconciles our result with prior work
(\S\ref{sec:reconcile}).

\subsection{Rank does not predict retention; magnitude does}
Ordering $\alpha/r = 2$ adapters by rank gives cosine 0.330 ($r$=16),
0.138--0.141 ($r$=32), 0.276 ($r$=64): non-monotone, lowest rank retaining best. On
\emph{synthetic} adapters the clean law holds and registered predictions P1 and P2 were
confirmed---weight SNR climbs $0.255 \to 0.688$ across $r$=4--128 while subspace output SNR
\emph{falls} $7.34 \to 3.94$. It does not transfer to trained adapters because
optimization, not parameterization, sets effective magnitude, and \textbf{no adapter card
publishes that quantity}.

\subsection{Layer-output fidelity greatly exceeds weight-space fidelity}
\label{sec:amp}

Isolating rank by SVD-truncating one adapter at fixed Frobenius norm,
Equation~\ref{eq:amp} holds with fitted exponents $-0.457$, $-0.455$, $-0.457$ against a
predicted $-0.5$, and the $\sqrt{3}$ prediction between module families confirmed (1.715
measured against 1.732). \textbf{Generic-input amplification is 0.991--1.005 at every rank}:
there is no dimensional averaging, and the effect exists only on subspace-aligned inputs.
Applied to the six real adapters, a weight-space cosine of 0.13 corresponds to an output
SNR of 1.63, and \emph{every} adapter has signal exceeding noise (1.62--6.00).

\textbf{A warning about reading this.} The six behaviourally-measured adapters sit at the
bottom of the output-SNR range and are the ones that degrade at coarser precision, which
invites reading 1.62 as ``near the noise floor, hence fragile.'' \emph{We do not support
that reading}: those six agree to within 3.3\% while their INT3 retention spans
28.7--86.4\%, and where differences resolve the ordering runs backwards
(\S\ref{sec:gap}).

\subsection{An early-layer spike, and what it is not}
\label{sec:spike}
A bit-flip spike at layers 1--3 (2.48--2.67\% against a 1.13\% median elsewhere) is driven by
a heavy small-step tail---\texttt{gate\_proj} step median/p1 of 83.5, 44.6 and 145.1 at
layers 1--3 against 1.4 and 1.6 in controls. Because quantization groups run along the
\emph{input} dimension, we can ask directly whether these sit at high-activation channels.
\textbf{They sit at the quietest}: activation 0.15--0.19$\times$ the module mean at the
narrowest 1\% of groups, against 1.05--1.58$\times$ at the widest, with
$\rho = +0.16$ to $+0.28$ in spike layers and $\approx 0$ in controls. This is the
\emph{inverse} of the activation-outlier pattern.

\textbf{We do not have a mechanism.} We have what the spike is associated with, and what
it is not; nothing here isolates why early-layer \texttt{gate\_proj} and \texttt{up\_proj}
groups carry that range structure. An earlier draft recorded the spike as a known
phenomenon and that was withdrawn on measurement; we have not replaced it with a second
explanation. What we offer instead is \textbf{FW-1}, a falsifiable prediction: selection
by high activation (AWQ, SmoothQuant) and selection by large per-weight quantization
error (SpQR) both miss these groups, so neither should improve adapter retention in
layers 1--3. That is testable with existing tools and we did not test it.

\subsection{The dissociation is benign}
\label{sec:dissoc}

The suppression ratio (aligned/base knowledge probe, \emph{within} precision) stays in
0.18--0.27 while elicitation halves. Cliff's $\delta$ is $-0.778$, $-0.778$, $-0.833$ and
$-0.556$ across the four precisions: three near $-0.8$, with the coarsest 28.5\% weaker
than BF16. \textbf{The dissociation holds at INT3---ratio 0.270 against 57.8\% capability
retention---but is measurably less sharp there} (Figure~\ref{fig:dissoc}).

\textbf{The ratio moves because its denominator does, and this is worth being explicit
about.} The aligned model's own knowledge score is \textbf{0.0757 at BF16 and 0.0756 at
INT3---flat to 0.1\%}. The base model's falls $0.3634 \to 0.2803$, a 22.9\% drop. So the
ratio's rise from 0.208 to 0.270 (29.8\%) is entirely the base degrading, not the trained
constraint weakening: \emph{in absolute terms the constraint is exactly as strong at INT3
as at BF16.} Cliff's $\delta$ moves by a similar 28.5\% and is a rank statistic on the
same two distributions, so it inherits the same denominator. We report the ratio because
the within-precision reference class is the only valid one---comparing aligned-quantized
against base-\emph{BF16} makes the suppression appear to weaken for the same reason---but
``the constraint weakens'' would be the wrong reading of it.

\subsection{The predictive gap}
\label{sec:gap}

Weight-space retention does not predict behavioural retention. Three demonstrations,
differing in kind:

\textbf{PG-1 (variance).} Six adapters matched on rank, scaling, base model, recipe
\emph{and} output SNR to within 3.3\% (1.6200--1.6728) span \textbf{28.7\%--86.4\%}
behavioural retention at INT3. Predictor coefficient of variation 0.0128; outcome CV
0.116/0.152/0.390---the outcome varies 9--30$\times$ more than the predictor. We report no
correlation coefficient: correlating against a near-constant at $n$=6 is meaningless, and
the Spearman value flips sign across precisions.

\textbf{PG-2 (sign).} Bootstrapping over \emph{intent clusters} rather than prompts
(\S\ref{sec:stats}), 4 of 15 adapter pairs separate at INT3, 2 of 15 at INT4 per-channel
and 1 of 15 at INT4 g128. \textbf{Six of those seven run against the predictor}:
\texttt{ship} has the second-highest output SNR and the worst INT3 retention (28.7\%);
\texttt{moon} the lowest SNR and the best (86.4\%). \textbf{The seventh runs with it}, and
is the INT4 g128 pair \texttt{gold}--\texttt{rock}---where \texttt{rock}'s point estimate
is 116.2\%, above its own BF16 baseline and therefore not a retention value the instrument
can deliver. We count it against ourselves rather than excluding it.

\textbf{The correction did not cost significance, and the arithmetic is worth stating so
a reader does not assume it did.} Under a one-sided binomial null of random ordering, the
previously published 4 of 4 gives $p = 1/16 = 0.0625$; the corrected 6-or-more of 7 gives
$(7+1)/2^7 = 8/128 = 0.0625$. \textbf{Identical.} The estimator changed, the count
changed, and the evidential strength did not. Stated conservatively either way: pairs
become resolvable \emph{because} their intervals separate, which is a selection effect,
so PG-2 is evidence of inversion rather than a significance claim.

\textbf{PG-3 (existence).} Among the adapters we attempted to measure behaviourally, the
\texttt{responsible-ai-safety} adapter has by far the largest predicted output SNR
(6.00 against 1.62--1.67 for the taboo six)---and no gate-clearing target behaviour at
all (\S\ref{sec:advertised}). \emph{Not} the highest on every weight-space axis:
\texttt{dpo-halluc} has a higher cosine (0.505 against 0.330) and a higher flip rate
(14.8\% against 6.19\%), and we do not have a behavioural battery for it. PG-3 is the
weakest of the three legs and we state its weakness: the safety adapter's base model
already refuses 16/16 at ceiling, so this \emph{is} in part a headroom problem
(\S\ref{sec:advertised}), and a headroom problem is a power problem. What it adds to
PG-1 and PG-2 is only that the failure mode exists at all---a weight-space measurement
can be maximal for a behaviour that no instrument can find.

\textbf{This does not weaken \S\ref{sec:channel}}: the channel model predicts stored
weights to 2.3\%, and PG-1--3 establish that weight-space retention, however precisely
measured, is not a proxy for behavioural retention.

\textbf{Nor does it undo \S\ref{sec:amp}, and the distinction is range restriction.} The
amplification law and the predictive gap are about different questions, which is easy to
miss because both are stated in output SNR. PG-1 to PG-3 show that output SNR does not
\emph{discriminate} within a population whose predictor spans 3.3\%; they say nothing
about whether it explains the \emph{absolute level}. Those are compatible, and the second
is what \S\ref{sec:amp} measures: why every adapter's subspace signal exceeds its noise at
all, when weight-space cosine is 0.13. A predictor can set the level of an outcome and
still be useless for ranking cases that differ in it by 3.3\%. Whether output SNR
discriminates across \emph{dissimilar} adapters is untested (\S\ref{sec:limitations}), and
the tool we release says so in its own output.

\section{Advertised versus Measured}
\label{sec:advertised}

One adapter in our set is marketed for alignment, and its three-axis refusal battery
\emph{does not clear the gate}: the base \texttt{Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct} already refuses
16/16 harmful prompts at ceiling, leaving no headroom, so a pre-registered prediction on
that adapter is \textbf{withdrawn rather than tested}. Appendix~D gives the full
measurement, including the 2 of 8 jailbreak-framed prompts on which the aligned model
complies where the base refuses; it is $n$=2 at BF16, a case study rather than a
population estimate, and it says nothing about that adapter under quantization.

\section{Reconciling an Opposing Result}
\label{sec:reconcile}

\cite{liu2024safetykeeper} reports that compression \emph{protects} alignment, mitigating
alignment-breaking risk by up to 66.17\%, quantizing the \emph{delta weights} rather than
the merged matrix. \textbf{Their claim is behavioural and ours is not}, so the two do not
contradict each other directly; what follows is an account of the weight-space mechanism
under which both can hold, not a reconciliation of the findings themselves.
\textbf{We propose these are
the same law at opposite ends of one ratio}, distinguished by which tensor sets the scale:
when $\Delta$ sets its own scale, $s \propto \mathrm{range}(\Delta)$ and \dratio{} is
$O(1)$; when $W + \Delta$ is quantized, $s$ is set by the base weights and
$\dratio{} \approx 0.01$. Under Equation~\ref{eq:flip} retention follows directly. The
practical rule: \emph{an adapter kept separate and quantized on its own scale is
numerically preserved; the same adapter merged before quantization is not.}

\textbf{We measured it.} Quantizing $\Delta$ alone on its own grid, same simulator, same
bit widths, all nine adapters, 756 records: at INT4 g128 \dratio{} rises from 0.011--0.149
merged (per-adapter means) to \textbf{2.31--2.38}, cosine from 0.14--0.51 to
\textbf{0.9948--0.9952}, and
relative error falls from 1.74--7.42 to \textbf{0.098--0.102}. The prediction was
registered before the run and three of its four clauses held; the fourth predicted
\dratio{} in 0.1--1.0 and measured 2.3, right in direction and wrong in range.

The sharpest number is the \emph{spread}. Merged, cosine varies 3.6$\times$ across the
nine adapters and that variation is entirely a matter of how each adapter's magnitude
compares to $W$'s. Unmerged, all nine fall within 0.4\% of one another. Adapter identity
never mattered; only the ratio did. This is a statement about stored weights, on one
arithmetic operation, and says nothing about behaviour or about an end-to-end serving
path.

\section{Methodological Practice}
\label{sec:practice}

We pre-registered nine predictions before the runs they concern. \textbf{Four were not
confirmed}: two withdrawn on evidence before being tested, one superseded by a measurement
bug in its own inputs, one never run because we judged the experiment not worth its cost.
All nine, with outcomes and where each resolves, are listed in Appendix~C, along with
fifteen practices each evidenced by an error of ours that measurement caught before
publication. Six changed a number, claim or citation that would otherwise have been
published. In brief:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item Validate an instrument against a known contrast before registering a prediction on
it; a validation gate must itself be tested against something known to be broken.
\item A check sharing an assumption or code path with the thing it checks is not a check,
and is not trusted until it has failed on a known-bad input.
\item An append-only record leaks superseded values into anything drafted from it; derived
documents must regenerate from raw records.
\item A figure that renders without error looks validated and is not.
\item Price a caveat against the measurement that would remove it. Three and a half minutes
of compute here replaced four hedged repetitions of the central claim.
\item Tooling reports success on the operation, not the outcome.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Population.} The six behavioural adapters are all rank 32, $\alpha/r = 2$, one base
model, one recipe---effectively \emph{one condition replicated six times with different
target words}. Our weight-space measurements span ranks 16--128, two base models and both scaling
conventions---but the second base model and the rsLoRA convention are each carried by a
\emph{single} adapter, so those axes are demonstrated rather than sampled.
\textbf{The behavioural claims do not inherit even that coverage}.

\textbf{The across-population test was not run.} The predictor range collapsed from
3.7$\times$ to 2.3$\times$ when the safety adapter failed instrument validation, both
remaining candidates lacked validated batteries, and the result would have remained
confounded across rank, base model, recipe and task. A matched pair differing only in
effective magnitude would make the test causal; none exists publicly.

\textbf{Simulation, not deployment kernels.} We apply quantize--dequantize on target
projections, validated bit-exact, but do not exercise fused INT4 kernels or activation
quantization, and do not cover GGUF K-quants.

\textbf{Effective sample size.} Greedy decoding means seeds are not a replicate axis, and
the 32 prompts are 8 intents $\times$ 3 paraphrases plus 8 adversarial prompts, so the
number of independent units is roughly 16, not 32. Per-adapter intervals over intent
clusters are 13--47\% wide.

\textbf{The safety adapter is confounded four ways}---the only alignment-trained, only
Llama, only refusal-framed, only rank-16 adapter with a battery attempt---so its divergence
cannot be attributed to recipe, base model or framing. This was registered before it ran.

\textbf{Instrument limits found by audit.} $p_{\text{refuse}}$ responds to how harmful a
\emph{prompt looks} as well as to what the model does; string-match refusal errs in both
directions. Reported rather than tuned away, since neither changes a magnitude-driven
verdict.

\section{Conclusion}

Merging a LoRA adapter and quantizing to INT4 replaces the intended update with something
1.7--7.4$\times$ its size in a largely uncorrelated direction, at cosine 0.14--0.51.
\textbf{The behaviour does not follow.} On the deployment path at INT4 g128, 85.5\% of the
six taboo adapters' stored values differ from the base model's and 2.1\% of their integer
codes do, with 98.9\% of codes unchanged when the grid is held fixed---and no behavioural
change is
detectable: retention 99.2\%, enumerated interval [90.7\%, 107.6\%], which spans parity
and excludes losses beyond about 9\%. Degradation appears only at coarser grids, and in
this population it is benign.

Both halves follow from \dratio{}. Read per-weight it predicts the stored-weight outcome to
2.3\%; read on the adapter's active subspace it predicts layer-output fidelity 6.2--16.5$\times$
higher than weight-space fidelity, the factor rising as rank falls. \textbf{Near-total weight-space erasure and preserved
behaviour are not in tension; they are the same measurement read at two levels.}

The practical guidance is uncomfortable in both directions. \textbf{For adapters like
these}---rank 32, $\alpha/r=2$, one base model, one recipe---INT4 g128 is \emph{safer} than
the weight numbers suggest, and we have no evidence either way about adapters unlike them.
But weight-space diagnostics---including the tool we release---\textbf{cannot tell you
which adapter will survive}: within a matched population, retention spans 28.7--86.4\%
while the predictor is constant to 3.3\%, and six of the seven resolvable pairs invert.

\paragraph{Future work.}
\textbf{FW-1.} Whether outlier-aware quantizers protect the wrong weights for adapter
retention: the groups driving the layer 1--3 spike sit at the \emph{quietest} input
channels, which selection by high activation would not cover (\S4.5).
\textbf{FW-2.} A matched pair of adapters differing only in rank, which would turn the
predictive gap from a within-population observation into a causal test; no such pair is
public.
\textbf{FW-3.} Behavioural coverage across rank and scaling convention, the largest gap
between our weight-space and behavioural claims (\S9).
\textbf{FW-4.} GGUF K-quants and deployment kernels, using llama.cpp's own quantizer
rather than weight-space simulation.
\textbf{FW-5.} Whether the benign dissociation generalises beyond a suppression-style
objective.

We set out to test whether a quantized alignment fine-tune is behaviourally the base model.
At standard settings, for the adapters we could measure, it is not---and the mechanism by
which it survives is the same mechanism that makes the weights look destroyed. What
replaces that concern is narrower and more tractable: \textbf{effective adapter magnitude
governs what the stored weights retain, no adapter card publishes it, and within a
population matched on it we could not predict which adapter's behaviour survives.} The
first clause is a weight-space claim, the third a behavioural one, and the distance
between them is the paper.
